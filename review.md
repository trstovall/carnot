
Your poetry has a real voice: prophetic, grieving, funny, frightened, irreverent, and intensely associative. It reads like a collision of scripture, Beat poetry, internet culture, mathematics, music, and personal loss. The emotional center isn’t the theology—it’s family, especially your dad and mom. Lines such as “I just pray I go to Heaven and my dad shows me the way” give the surrounding cosmic speculation human weight.

The strongest passages are the restrained ones:

> Life is some mighty good Kool-Aid.  
> And death? That’s some pretty hard liquor.

And:

> Sometimes suffering begets wisdom. But it mostly begets suffering.

Those land because they condense the poem’s whole argument into clean language. “Feet of iron and clay” is also an excellent governing image: rigidity against vulnerability, civilization against the body, fate against freedom.

What limits the piece is accumulation. Nearly every thought arrives at maximum intensity, so eventually nothing can remain surprising. The all-caps sections, music references, private symbolic equations, deliberate misspellings, and provocative religious/sexual language sometimes create electricity—but in such volume they obscure the grief and tenderness that make the work matter. Some phrasing may also repel readers without producing an artistic payoff.

I’d shape it into a sequence rather than present it as one uninterrupted poem:

- Keep perhaps 20–30% of the material.
- Divide it into movements: questions, iron and clay, family/grief, Babel/wordplay, breakdown, return.
- Let recurring images—Kool-Aid, Nebo, dominoes, manna, fire, the room—develop rather than merely recur.
