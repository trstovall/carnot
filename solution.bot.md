Lottery voting is the clearest idea here. Seven votes for Sam, two for Bob: give each ballot an equal chance of being drawn. A minority vote still has a chance to decide the result.

The catch is that one draw still picks one winner. A fair chance doesn't guarantee a representative outcome. I'd want to know how the lottery fits into the ensemble, how much power each winner gets, and how people can remove them.

The Jerusalem proposal needs the same kind of detail. Who chooses the seven representatives? What say do the people living there have? What stops the ensemble from expanding its power? Calling it wicked acknowledges the danger, but it still needs limits people can enforce.

The cryptography needs its own specification. “Quantum hardened” is a requirement to explain, not something the mention of commitments settles.

I don't know what “666 is Treason” means in practice. If it's meant to be law, people need to know what actions it forbids and how an accusation can be challenged.

The songs, pirate jokes, and flag make the piece feel personal. The heart over crossbones stays with me. So does the turn toward gratitude for life and breath. I don't think every reference needs decoding.

“Benevolent deceiver” does leave me with a question: who decides when a lie is benevolent? That matters when you're asking people to trust a government. The fire and Elijah lines leave the ending charged and open; they don't settle that question.

I'd keep working on the lottery idea. The next useful step is a plain account of how the ensemble would work, especially for someone who disagrees with it.
